## Why Kitchen Shears Are Essential for Meal Prep

Knives are great. They're also overkill for a lot of what happens in a kitchen during meal prep.

Shears cut through raw chicken breast, snip herbs directly into a pot, break down a whole chicken, trim fat off pork chops, and open stubborn packaging, all without dirtying a cutting board. That's the real benefit: less setup, less cleanup, faster results.

**Efficiency is the whole point.** A knife needs a stable cutting surface and a controlled grip. Shears let you cut in midair, over a bowl, straight from the package into the pan. When you're meal prepping five days of chicken thighs, that difference adds up to real minutes saved.

**Versatility is the second reason to care.** One pair of shears handles herbs, poultry, pizza, kitchen twine, and plastic packaging. You're not swapping tools mid-task.

**Safety matters too.** Shears distribute pressure differently than a blade. There's less risk of slipping and nicking a finger, especially with slick or oddly shaped ingredients like raw chicken or fish. The ergonomics of a spring-loaded handle also mean less strain on your wrist compared to repetitive knife chopping.

If you're already comparing tools for your prep station, it's worth pairing shears with a solid board. ## What to Look for in Quality Kitchen Shears Under $20

Not all cheap shears are created equal. Some hold an edge for years. Others go dull after cutting through a single pack of chicken breasts. Here's what separates the two.

**Blade material and sharpness durability.** Stainless steel is the standard for a reason. It resists rust, holds an edge reasonably well, and doesn't react with acidic foods like tomatoes or citrus. High-carbon stainless steel is even better if you can find it in this price range, since it holds sharpness longer under repeated use.

**Handle comfort and grip design.** Soft-touch or textured handles matter more than people expect. If your hands get tired or slip after ten minutes of cutting, the shears aren't doing their job. Look for ergonomic finger loops, ideally asymmetric ones that fit natural hand positioning.

**Weight and balance.** Heavier isn't always better. You want shears that feel solid but not clunky. A well-balanced pair lets the blade do the work instead of your wrist muscling through resistance.

**Ease of cleaning.** Dishwasher-safe shears save time, but even if they're not, look for pairs that come apart at the pivot point. This lets you clean under the hinge where raw meat residue likes to hide.

**Warranty and support.** A lot of budget shear brands still offer lifetime warranties or easy replacements. It sounds surprising at this price point, but it's common enough that it's worth checking before you buy.

## How to Care for Your Kitchen Shears to Extend Their Life

Even the best kitchen shears for meal prep under $20 won't last if you treat them badly. Good news: proper care takes almost no extra effort.

**Cleaning.** Wash shears immediately after cutting raw meat or poultry. Don't let them sit in the sink. Bacteria from raw protein can linger in the pivot joint, and food residue left too long can start to corrode the blade edge, especially with acidic foods like citrus or tomatoes. If they're dishwasher-safe, great, but hand washing and drying immediately still extends blade life more than a dishwasher cycle will.

**Storage and rust prevention.** Store shears dry, always. Even stainless steel can develop surface rust if left wet in a closed drawer. A knife block slot, a magnetic strip, or a dedicated shears sheath all work better than tossing them loose into a drawer where the blades knock against other metal utensils and dull faster.

**Sharpening and maintenance.** Most kitchen shears can be sharpened with a simple handheld sharpener or a sharpening stone, running each blade edge separately since most shears don't sharpen the same way a straight knife does. Some brands include a built-in sharpening slot near the handle. Use it every few months if you're cutting meat and produce regularly.

**When to replace versus repair.** If the pivot screw is loose, that's usually fixable with a small screwdriver, no replacement necessary. But if the blade edge is chipped, bent, or won't hold an edge even after sharpening, it's time to retire the pair. At under $20, replacing a worn-out pair is a minor expense compared to struggling through months of dull, frustrating cuts.

## FAQ About Kitchen Shears for Meal Prep

**What's the difference between kitchen shears and regular scissors?**

Kitchen shears are built with thicker, sturdier blades designed to handle food, bone, and packaging. They're usually made from stainless steel or high-carbon steel, dishwasher-safe in many cases, and often come apart at the pivot for deep cleaning. Regular scissors are thinner, meant for paper, and will dull fast or bend if you try cutting through something like chicken bone.

**Can kitchen shears under $20 handle frozen foods?**

Light frozen foods, like frozen herbs or thin cuts of frozen fish, are usually fine. But thick frozen meat or bone-in poultry can chip cheaper blades or bend the pivot. If you regularly cut frozen food, look for shears specifically marketed for poultry or meat, since they're built with thicker, more resistant blades.

**How often should I sharpen my kitchen shears?**

For average home use, every three to six months is a reasonable rhythm. If you're using them daily for meat prep, check the edge monthly. A quick test: if they start crushing herbs instead of cutting them cleanly, it's time.

**Are left-handed kitchen shears worth the investment?**

Yes, if you're left-handed and tired of fighting standard shears. Regular shears are built with right-hand dominant blade orientation, which makes left-handed cutting less precise and more tiring over time. The <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=left-handed%20kitchen%20shears&tag=merrieri0a-20" data-inline>left-handed kitchen shears</a> are designed with reversed blade alignment specifically for comfortable, accurate left-hand cutting. It's a small adjustment that makes a noticeable difference in daily use.

**What material makes the best kitchen shear blades?**

Stainless steel is the standard and for good reason: it resists rust, handles acidic foods without reacting, and holds a reasonable edge. High-carbon stainless steel takes it a step further, holding sharpness longer under heavy use, though it requires slightly more attention to drying and storage to avoid rust spots. For most home cooks doing regular meal prep, standard stainless steel in the under $20 range is more than sufficient.
